Diffusion Models

Diffusion models, the technique behind AI image generators such as Stable Diffusion, apply almost everything on this site. An SDE turns data into noise (Chapter 5). A score describes the noised data. A reverse-time SDE turns noise back into brand-new data. Here the "data" is one-dimensional: numbers drawn from two bumps. An image model does the same with every pixel at once.

7.1Forward: Data to Noise

A diffusion model learns to create data by first watching how data is destroyed. Take the data samples (here, numbers clustered in two bumps) and let each one follow an SDE that slowly erases it into pure random noise.

Predict first: By t = 3, what will the histogram look like?

Scrub the time or press Play. The black curve is the exact distribution of the noised data. The dashed curve is pure noise.

$$dX_t = -X_t\,dt + \sqrt2\,dW_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_t = e^{-t}X_0 + \sqrt{1 - e^{-2t}}\;Z$$
$X_0$
One data sample: a number from one of the two bumps.
$X_t$
The same sample after being noised for time $t$.
$-X_t\,dt$
A drift pulling every sample toward 0. This is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process of section 5.2.
$\sqrt2\,dW_t$
Random kicks. The $\sqrt2$ is chosen so the pull and the kicks balance at a long-run spread of exactly 1.
$Z$
A fresh standard bell-curve number, drawn from $N(0, 1)$.
$e^{-t}$
How much of the original sample survives: 37% at $t = 1$, 5% at $t = 3$.

In plain words: noising is "shrink a little, add a little noise", repeated. The formula on the right shows the result can be computed in one jump: keep a fraction $e^{-t}$ of the sample and add bell-curve noise. After enough time, where a sample started no longer matters, and every dataset ends up as the same featureless $N(0,1)$. That direction is easy. The useful direction is the reverse: start from noise and produce new data. That is what sections 7.2 and 7.3 build.

Left: 40 of the 3,000 samples being noised, up to the current time. Right: histogram of all samples now (blue), the exact noised distribution (black), and pure noise N(0, 1) (dashed).

The forward process needs no learning: any data, however complicated, becomes the same $N(0,1)$ noise. In an image model this happens to every pixel at once, and the end result looks like TV static.

Going deeper: the precise statement

The general forward SDE. Song et al. (2021) use the "variance-preserving" SDE $dX = -\tfrac12\beta(t)X\,dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dW$ with a noise schedule $\beta(t)$. This page is the constant case $\beta = 2$. Its solution is $X_t = a_t X_0 + \sqrt{1 - a_t^2}\,Z$ with $a_t = e^{-\frac12\int_0^t \beta(s)\,ds}$.

The discrete version (DDPM). Ho, Jain & Abbeel (2020) use steps $x_k = \sqrt{1 - \beta_k}\,x_{k-1} + \sqrt{\beta_k}\,z_k$, a discretisation of the same SDE (compare Euler–Maruyama, section 5.1). The idea of learning to reverse a noising process goes back to Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015).

References: J. Sohl-Dickstein et al., ICML (2015). J. Ho, A. Jain & P. Abbeel, NeurIPS (2020). Y. Song et al., "Score-Based Generative Modeling through Stochastic Differential Equations", ICLR (2021).

Show the code (Python + NumPy) 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(20)
w, m, s = np.array([0.35, 0.65]), np.array([-2.0, 1.5]), np.array([0.3, 0.5])   # the "data": two bumps
k = rng.choice(2, size=100_000, p=w)
x0 = rng.normal(m[k], s[k])
for t in (0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 3.0):
    xt = np.exp(-t) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - np.exp(-2 * t)) * rng.normal(size=x0.size)   # exact noising to time t
    print(f"t = {t}: mean {xt.mean():+.3f}, spread {xt.std():.3f}, share left of the valley {np.mean(xt < -0.25):.3f}")
# pure noise N(0, 1): mean 0, spread 1, share left of -0.25 = 0.401

7.2The Score

To run the movie backward, one thing is needed at every noise level $t$ and every position $x$: which direction makes a sample more likely, and how strongly. That is the score. The top panel shows the noised density at the chosen $t$. The bottom panel shows its score, as a curve and as arrows.

Predict first: Drag t up to a lot of noise. What will the score become?

Drag the noise level. Near $t = 0$ the arrows point into each bump, and the valley between them pushes samples out toward one bump or the other.

$$s(t, x) \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\log p(t, x) \;=\; \frac{\partial p(t,x)/\partial x}{p(t, x)}$$
$p(t, x)$
The density of the noised data at time $t$ (section 5.3): the black curve of section 7.1, and the blue curve here.
$\log p$
The natural logarithm of the density. Taking the log makes the score equally informative in crowded and sparse regions.
$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$
The slope as $x$ changes, with $t$ held fixed (section 5.3).
$s(t, x)$
The score. Positive means "more likely to the right", negative means "more likely to the left", and its size says how steeply.

In plain words: the score is a compass pointing uphill on the density. Because it is the slope divided by the height, it stays strong even far out where the density is tiny: a lost sample always knows the way home. With little noise, the compass points into the nearest bump. With a lot of noise, everything looks like one bell curve and the compass simply points to 0. A real diffusion model does not know $p$. It trains a neural network to predict the score from noisy examples. Here the data is a known pair of bumps, so the score can be computed exactly.

Knowing the score at every noise level is as good as knowing the data distribution itself. And a neural network can learn it from examples alone, by learning to denoise: the Going deeper panel explains why denoising and the score are the same thing.

Going deeper: the precise statement

Tweedie's formula: the score is a denoiser. For $X_t = a X_0 + \sqrt{1 - a^2}\,Z$ with $a = e^{-t}$: $\;\mathbb E[X_0 \mid X_t = x] = \big(x + (1 - a^2)\,s(t, x)\big)/a$. The best guess of the clean sample is the noisy one, nudged along the score and scaled back up.

Denoising score matching. A network $s_\theta(t, x)$ is trained to minimise $\mathbb E\,\big\lvert s_\theta(t, X_t) + \tfrac{X_t - aX_0}{1 - a^2} \big\rvert^2$, averaged over data $X_0$, times $t$ and noise. The target uses only the known noise that was added, yet the minimiser is the true score $s(t, x)$ (Vincent, 2011), a consequence of Tweedie's formula. This is how every diffusion model is trained.

References: A. Hyvärinen, J. Machine Learning Research 6 (2005), 695–709. P. Vincent, Neural Computation 23 (2011), 1661–1674. B. Efron, "Tweedie's formula and selection bias", J. Amer. Statist. Assoc. 106 (2011), 1602–1614.

Show the code (Python + NumPy) 
import numpy as np
w, m, s = np.array([0.35, 0.65]), np.array([-2.0, 1.5]), np.array([0.3, 0.5])

def score(t, x):                                     # d/dx log p(t, x) for the noised two-bump data
    mean, var = m * np.exp(-t), s ** 2 * np.exp(-2 * t) + 1 - np.exp(-2 * t)
    dens = w * np.exp(-(x - mean) ** 2 / (2 * var)) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * var)
    return np.sum(dens * -(x - mean) / var) / np.sum(dens)

for t in (0.05, 1.0, 5.0):
    print(f"t = {t}: " + "  ".join(f"s({x:+.0f}) = {score(t, x):+.2f}" for x in (-3, 0, 3)))
print("pure noise N(0, 1) has score -x: +3, 0, -3")

7.3Noise to Data: the Reverse-Time SDE

Now start from pure noise and run time backward. Anderson's theorem (1982) says the backward-running process is again an SDE, with one new ingredient: the score. Every particle starts as a random $N(0,1)$ number at $t = 3$ and is steered, step by step, back to $t = 0$.

Predict first: Delete the score term from the reverse equation. What will the particles end up as?

Choose a method. The paths run from noise (left, $t = 3$) to generated samples (right, $t = 0$). The histogram shows where all 3,000 particles end, against the true data (black).

$$dX_t = \big[-X_t - 2\,s(t, X_t)\big]\,dt + \sqrt2\,d\overline{W}_t \qquad \text{(run with time going backward, } t: 3 \to 0\text{)}$$
time going backward
Each step moves $t$ down by a small amount, so $dt$ is negative.
$-X_t\,dt$
The forward drift of section 7.1. Run backward it pushes away from 0.
$-2\,s(t, X_t)\,dt$
The score correction. Because $dt$ is negative, it moves each particle along the score arrows of section 7.2, toward more likely places.
$d\overline{W}_t$
Fresh random kicks for the backward run.
probability-flow ODE
The same equation with half the score term and no noise: $dX = [-X - s(t, X)]\,dt$. Each noise sample then maps to one data sample, with no randomness along the way.

In plain words: playing the forward movie backward naively goes wrong. The pull toward 0 becomes a push away from 0, and the noise keeps spreading. The score term is precisely the correction that makes the backward movie statistically identical to the forward one played in reverse. It gathers the noise into the right bumps, in the right proportions. The ODE version reaches the same distribution along smooth, deterministic paths, using the Fokker–Planck equation of section 5.3 to trade randomness for drift.

With the score, pure noise becomes new samples with the right bumps, in the right proportions (35% and 65%), at the right places and widths, without copying a single data point. This is how diffusion image generators draw a picture. They start from static and follow the reverse-time SDE (or its ODE) with a neural-network score, in millions of dimensions instead of one.

Going deeper: the precise statement

Anderson's theorem (1982). If $dX = f(t, X)\,dt + g(t)\,dW$ has densities $p_t$, then the same process run backward in time solves $dX = \big[f(t, X) - g(t)^2\,\partial_x \log p_t(X)\big]dt + g(t)\,d\overline W$, where $\overline W$ is a Brownian motion running backward. Here $f = -x$ and $g = \sqrt2$, which gives the equation above.

Probability-flow ODE. The ODE $\dot x = f - \tfrac12 g^2\,\partial_x \log p_t$ has the same densities $p_t$ at every time. Substituting it into the Fokker–Planck equation (section 5.3) shows its drift term reproduces the diffusion term exactly. It enables fast deterministic sampling and exact likelihoods.

In practice. The score is a trained network. Sampling uses a few dozen to a thousand steps of an SDE or ODE solver, often in a compressed "latent" space (Stable Diffusion) and steered by a text prompt ("guidance").

References: B. Anderson, "Reverse-time diffusion equation models", Stoch. Proc. Appl. 12 (1982), 313–326. Song et al., ICLR (2021). R. Rombach et al., "High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models", CVPR (2022).

Show the code (Python + NumPy) 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
w, m, s = np.array([0.35, 0.65]), np.array([-2.0, 1.5]), np.array([0.3, 0.5])

def score(t, x):                                     # exact score of the noised data, for many x at once
    mean, var = m * np.exp(-t), s ** 2 * np.exp(-2 * t) + 1 - np.exp(-2 * t)
    dens = w * np.exp(-(x[:, None] - mean) ** 2 / (2 * var)) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * var)
    return np.sum(dens * -(x[:, None] - mean) / var, axis=1) / np.sum(dens, axis=1)

T, steps = 3.0, 600
h = T / steps
x = rng.normal(size=20_000)                          # start from pure noise
for i in range(steps):                               # run time backward from T to 0
    x = x + h * (x + 2 * score(T - i * h, x)) + np.sqrt(2 * h) * rng.normal(size=x.size)
left = x < -0.25
print(f"left bump {left.mean():.3f} (data 0.35); centres {x[left].mean():.2f}, {x[~left].mean():.2f} (data -2, 1.5)")
print(f"widths {x[left].std():.2f}, {x[~left].std():.2f} (data 0.30, 0.50)")

Output when we ran it:

widths 0.31, 0.50 (data 0.30, 0.50)
