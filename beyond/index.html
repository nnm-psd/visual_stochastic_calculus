Beyond Brownian Motion

Brownian motion is the backbone of this site, but real randomness also jumps, and real signals are usually seen only through noise. This chapter covers two classic extensions: processes with jumps, and filtering a hidden signal out of noisy measurements.

8.1Jumps

Brownian paths never jump: over a short time they can only move a little (section 1.3). Market crashes, insurance claims and spikes in neural activity do jump. The simplest fix adds jumps at random moments: a Poisson process decides when, and each jump has a random size.

Predict first: Keep the total variance fixed, but put most of it into rare, big jumps. Compared with a bell curve, the distribution of end values will…

The total variance at $t = 1$ is fixed at 1. The sliders set how often jumps happen and what share of the variance they carry. The right panel uses a log scale, so rare extreme values stay visible next to the bell curve.

$$X_t = \sigma W_t + \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} J_k, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_t) = \big(\sigma^2 + \lambda\,\mathbb E[J^2]\big)\,t, \qquad [X]_t = \sigma^2 t + \sum_{k=1}^{N_t} J_k^2$$
$N_t$
A Poisson process: the number of jumps that have happened by time $t$. Jumps arrive at random moments, on average $\lambda$ per unit of time, independently of each other.
$\lambda$
The jump rate ("lambda"): how many jumps per unit of time on average.
$J_k$
The size of jump number $k$: here a bell-curve number with mean 0, so jumps go up or down.
$\sigma W_t$
The ordinary Brownian part, scaled by $\sigma$.
$\operatorname{Var}(X_t)$
The variance: the Brownian part contributes $\sigma^2 t$ and the jumps contribute $\lambda\,\mathbb E[J^2]\,t$.
$[X]_t$
The quadratic variation (section 2.1). Now it is random: $\sigma^2 t$ plus the sum of the squared jumps.
log scale
Each step up the vertical axis multiplies by 10, so a bar ten times shorter is still easy to see.

In plain words: a few big jumps and a steady Brownian wobble can have the same variance but look very different. With rare big jumps, most paths are quiet, but the ones that jump land far away. That is a fat tail: extreme outcomes are many times more common than a bell curve predicts. Push the jump rate up while keeping the variance fixed, and the jumps get smaller and more frequent until the process looks Brownian again (the central limit theorem at work). One more change from Brownian motion: the quadratic variation is no longer simply $t$, because each jump adds its square.

Jumps create fat tails: the same variance, far more extreme outcomes. Kurtosis (the average fourth power, 3 for a bell curve) measures it, and here it equals $3 + 3s^2/\lambda$, where $s$ is the share of variance from jumps. Rare big jumps give high kurtosis, while frequent small ones bring it back toward 3. That is why option markets price crash risk above what Black–Scholes predicts (section 6.1).

Going deeper: the precise statement

Lévy processes. Processes with independent, identically distributed increments are called Lévy processes. The Lévy–Itô decomposition splits every one of them into a drift, a Brownian part and a (compensated) sum of jumps, so this section's model is a typical member of the family.

Itô's formula with jumps. For $dX = \sigma\,dW + dJ$: $$f(X_t) = f(X_0) + \int_0^t f'(X_{s})\,\sigma\,dW_s + \tfrac12\int_0^t f''(X_s)\,\sigma^2\,ds + \sum_{s \le t} \big[f(X_s) - f(X_{s-})\big].$$ The continuous part follows Chapter 4. Each jump adds its full change in $f$, not a Taylor approximation.

In finance. Merton's jump-diffusion model (1976) adds normally distributed log-price jumps to Black–Scholes. The hedging argument of section 6.1 then breaks down, because a jump can't be hedged with the stock alone.

References: R. Merton, "Option pricing when underlying stock returns are discontinuous", J. Financial Economics 3 (1976), 125–144. R. Cont & P. Tankov, Financial Modelling with Jump Processes (Chapman & Hall/CRC, 2004). Shreve, Stochastic Calculus for Finance II, ch. 11.

Show the code (Python + NumPy) 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(23)
lam, share, paths = 2.0, 0.8, 200_000                # 2 jumps per unit time carry 80% of the variance
sigma, delta = np.sqrt(1 - share), np.sqrt(share / lam)
n = rng.poisson(lam, paths)                          # number of jumps by t = 1, per path
X1 = sigma * rng.normal(size=paths) + delta * np.sqrt(n) * rng.normal(size=paths)   # sum of n N(0, delta^2) jumps
print(f"variance {X1.var():.3f} (1)   kurtosis {np.mean(X1 ** 4) / X1.var() ** 2:.2f} (theory {3 + 3 * share ** 2 / lam:.2f}; bell curve 3)")
print(f"share of |X1| > 3: {np.mean(np.abs(X1) > 3):.4f}   (bell curve 0.0027)")

Output when we ran it:

variance 1.006 (1)   kurtosis 3.97 (theory 3.96; bell curve 3)
share of |X1| > 3: 0.0079   (bell curve 0.0027)

8.2The Kalman–Bucy Filter

Often the process you care about is hidden: a satellite's true position, a patient's true blood sugar, the "fair" value behind a noisy price. You only get noisy measurements of it. Filtering means estimating the hidden process, as it moves, from everything measured so far. For linear models like this one, the best possible estimate is itself the solution of an SDE: the Kalman–Bucy filter.

Predict first: Make the measurement noise r much larger. The filter's estimate will…

The hidden signal (thick grey) is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (section 5.2). The blue dots are noisy readings of it. The orange line is the filter's running estimate, and the shaded band is its own measure of uncertainty. Drag the measurement noise.

$$dm_t = -\theta\,m_t\,dt + \frac{P_t}{r^2}\big(dY_t - m_t\,dt\big), \qquad \frac{dP_t}{dt} = -2\theta P_t + \sigma^2 - \frac{P_t^2}{r^2}$$
$X_t$
The hidden signal, following $dX = -\theta X\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ with $\theta = \sigma = 1$.
$dY_t$
What is measured in a tiny time step: $X_t\,dt + r\,dV_t$, the signal plus independent noise $dV$.
$r$
How noisy the measurements are (the slider).
$m_t$
The filter's estimate: the best guess of $X_t$ given every measurement up to time $t$.
$P_t$
The variance of the estimate's error: how unsure the filter is. The band is $m_t \pm 2\sqrt{P_t}$.
$dY_t - m_t\,dt$
The innovation: the part of the new measurement that the estimate did not predict, the "surprise".
$P_t / r^2$
The Kalman gain: how much of the surprise to believe. It is large when the filter is unsure ($P$ big) or the data are clean ($r$ small).

In plain words: between measurements, the estimate moves the way the signal is expected to move (the $-\theta m\,dt$ term). When a measurement arrives, the estimate is nudged toward it, by an amount that weighs the filter's own uncertainty against the measurement's noise. The second equation tracks that uncertainty. It grows because the hidden signal keeps getting random kicks ($\sigma^2$), and shrinks because measurements keep arriving ($-P^2/r^2$), until the two balance. The estimate is then more accurate than any single reading, because it combines all of them with the model.

The filter beats the raw readings and knows its own uncertainty: the truth stays inside its band about 95% of the time, as a correct $\pm 2$-standard-deviation band should. Kalman filtering runs inside GPS receivers, spacecraft navigation (it flew on Apollo), weather forecasting and economic nowcasting.

Going deeper: the precise statement

Optimality. For linear signal and observation equations with Gaussian noise, the conditional distribution of $X_t$ given the observations up to $t$ is exactly normal, with mean $m_t$ and variance $P_t$. No other estimator built from the same data has a smaller mean squared error.

The innovation is Brownian motion. The scaled innovations $\tfrac1r(dY_t - m_t\,dt)$ form a Brownian motion. The filter extracts everything predictable from the data, and what remains is pure, independent noise.

Beyond linear models. For nonlinear models the conditional distribution is no longer normal. It solves a stochastic PDE (the Kushner–Stratonovich or Zakai equation), and in practice it is approximated by the extended Kalman filter or by particle filters.

References: R. Kalman, "A new approach to linear filtering and prediction problems", J. Basic Engineering 82 (1960), 35–45. R. Kalman & R. Bucy, "New results in linear filtering and prediction theory", J. Basic Engineering 83 (1961), 95–108. Øksendal, Stochastic Differential Equations, ch. 6.

Show the code (Python + NumPy) 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(24)
theta, sigma, r, T, steps = 1.0, 1.0, 0.5, 10.0, 2000
dt = T / steps
x = m = 0.0
P = sigma ** 2 / (2 * theta)                         # no information yet
err = []
for _ in range(steps):
    dY = x * dt + r * np.sqrt(dt) * rng.normal()     # noisy observation of the hidden signal
    m += -theta * m * dt + (P / r ** 2) * (dY - m * dt)    # Kalman-Bucy update of the estimate
    P += (-2 * theta * P + sigma ** 2 - P ** 2 / r ** 2) * dt   # Riccati equation for its variance
    x += -theta * x * dt + sigma * np.sqrt(dt) * rng.normal()   # the hidden signal moves on
    err.append(m - x)
p_inf = r ** 2 * (np.sqrt(theta ** 2 + sigma ** 2 / r ** 2) - theta)
print(f"filter error spread {np.std(err):.3f}   theory sqrt(P_inf) = {np.sqrt(p_inf):.3f}   no-data guess {np.sqrt(0.5):.3f}")

Output when we ran it:

filter error spread 0.506   theory sqrt(P_inf) = 0.556   no-data guess 0.707
